Executive Summary

Thirteen conclusions. If you read nothing else in this document, read these — everything that follows is the evidence and the plan behind them.

Read the full chapterCollapse chapter
  1. You have inherited a growth company with a returns problem. Revenue crossed ₹20,290 crore in FY26, up 15%1, with 13% underlying volume growth in the India branded business — genuinely best-in-class. But return on capital employed is 9.2% against a three-year ROE of 7.5%9, while the stock trades at roughly 60× earnings. At a cost of capital of approximately 11% — risk-free ~6.75%, equity risk premium ~6%, staples beta ~0.7, negligible debt Derived — a 9.2% ROCE means the company has been destroying value for three years, at roughly ₹450 crore a year. The plan's FY31 statutory 13–14% delivers a thin positive spread of about 250bps. The market is paying a compounder multiple for a business that has not yet cleared its hurdle rate. Closing that gap — not adding revenue — is the single largest source of value you control. WACC derived in section 09
  2. The core is a cash engine in a slow category — which is not the same as a deteriorating business. Tea and salt still generate most of the India profit pool. Salt is superb: ~39% share2, five straight quarters of double-digit growth, and a credible path past 40%. Tea reads worse than it is. Volumes grew 2% in a category compounding at under 3%, which means the business is holding or gaining share; the 4% revenue decline3 is deflation passed through to consumers in a year when input costs fell — correct pricing behaviour, not lost competitiveness. The real constraint on tea is the category's growth rate, and no amount of execution changes that. You cannot build a number-one food company on a category growing at 3% — but you must not damage the one you have, because it funds the plan and holds the shelf. Section 13
  3. The growth portfolio is working and is the real story. Sampann, Capital Foods, Organic India, Soulfull and ready-to-drink together grew 47% in Q1 FY2723 and now make up 36% of the India business, up from 28% a year ago. Management's own aspiration is ~45% within three to four years. This portfolio is the machine; your job is to feed it capital, talent and shelf space faster than the organisation is comfortable with.
  4. You are absent from four of India's largest and fastest food profit pools. Salty snacks (₹46,571 crore, doubling by 2033), dairy value-added products, biscuits and bakery, and chocolate/confectionery. Together these represent well over ₹2 lakh crore of consumer spend where Tata Consumer has effectively zero share — despite owning the distribution, the trust and the pantry adjacency to compete.
  5. "Number one" is the wrong primary objective — the objective is economic profit, and revenue rank is its consequence. Conclusion 8 shows that chasing ₹42,500 crore drives statutory ROCE to 13–14% rather than 18%. That is not a measurement problem to be reconciled; it is the plan telling you the two goals point in opposite directions. Set the primary objective as economic profit — (ROCE − WACC) × capital employed, which is negative ₹457 crore today — with revenue leadership as the expected by-product. Then define the rank honestly: the right benchmark is Nestlé, not ITC's whole FMCG business. Amul, at over ₹1 lakh crore, is structurally unreachable as a cooperative.26 The winnable target is number one among branded, listed F&B businesses. The bar is Nestlé India's domestic business, which reaches roughly ₹40,700 crore by FY31. ITC's often-quoted ₹40,790 crore includes personal care, agarbatti and stationery; ITC Foods lands nearer ₹35,250 crore. Measured correctly, leadership needs about ₹42,500 crore — a 16% CAGR, not 18% — which is roughly ₹4,000 crore of acquisitions you do not have to fund. But note what the bar depends on: Nestlé grew 14.9% last year, and at a 15% CAGR the bar moves to ₹46,400 crore and the required CAGR to 18%. A target that swings ₹4,000 crore on two points of a competitor's performance is a number to report, not to manage to — so manage to the absolute ₹42,500 crore, and to the spread over cost of capital beneath it, and restate rank annually. Sections 12 and 17
  6. Even so, the base case finishes second by revenue — and the cheapest fix is four points of growth-portfolio CAGR, not a larger cheque. Building the FY31 revenue walk from the disclosed segments, executing this entire plan competently lands at ₹39,800 crore, about ₹800 crore short of Nestlé. Leadership requires the growth portfolio to compound at 32% rather than 28%, or acquisitions bought at 3.2× rather than 3.5×. Four percentage points of growth-portfolio CAGR is worth more than doubling the M&A budget — and it is cheaper, faster, lower-risk and, unlike acquisitions, it raises returns instead of diluting them. That is why it is the right lever on both objectives, and where your attention belongs. The organic lever is the only one that improves rank and economic profit at the same time.
  7. Roughly two-thirds of the gap must be organic — you cannot buy your way to number one. Retained cash plus prudent leverage gives you ₹15,000–17,000 crore of firepower over five years if the dividend payout comes down from 64%. At 3–5× revenue, that buys ₹4,000–5,000 crore of acquired revenue, which compounds to roughly ₹8,400 crore of FY31 revenue. The rest has to come from the growth portfolio compounding above 30% and from stabilising tea. Any strategy that leads with acquisitions is the wrong strategy.
  8. The 18% ROCE target and the revenue target are arithmetically incompatible — and the shortfall is a decade-long condition, not a first-year effect. An asset bought at 3.5× revenue enters your capital base at 3.5× revenue; to earn 18% on that it would need a 63% EBIT margin. The tempting reading is that this is a year-one problem that growth fixes quickly. It is not. Run your own inputs forward: ₹15,000 crore at 3.5× buys ₹4,286 crore of revenue; compounding at 25% for three years gives ₹8,371 crore — the bridge figure in section 17 — which at a category-normal 14% EBITDA, less D&A and tax, is about ₹753 crore post-tax on ₹15,000 crore of capital: 5.0%, in year five. On unbroken 25% compounding it crosses an 11% cost of capital around FY34–35. Deploy the money and statutory ROCE reaches roughly 13% by FY31, not 18%, while the same business excluding acquisition goodwill earns 19%. Both numbers are true. Run both openly — statutory for the market, operating ex-goodwill for the operators — with the incentive split set out in section 19 and an annual goodwill return test. Do this before the snacks deal, when it reads as discipline, not after, when it reads as an excuse.
  9. Your margin ambition fights your growth ambition, and the honest landing is 16.5%, not 18%. Salty snacks earns 12–16% at scale. Quick commerce pays roughly nine points less contribution than general trade — a 100bps drag as it goes from 4% to 15% of the business. Acquisitions dilute for two years before they accrete. Every one of those is the right move and every one costs margin. Setting an 18% target guarantees a miss, and worse, invites the organisation to hit it by underinvesting in exactly the servicing frequency and brand support the growth plan depends on.
  10. Distribution is your most under-used asset and your most urgent fix. You reach 4.5 million outlets and 290 million households, yet Tata Water Plus is properly distributed in only 40–50% of the country3, and the Capital Foods and Organic India go-to-market rebuild is still short of distributors and salesmen. The gap between reach and effective reach is where several thousand crore of revenue is sitting.
  11. Channel economics are being rewritten under you. General trade is now 56% of your business — management has stopped quoting Nielsen share because the measurement no longer captures the market. Quick commerce is compounding at 70–80% and already takes 70–75% of e-grocery14. This is not a channel decision; it is a pack architecture, pricing, assortment and supply-chain decision that touches every P&L line.
  12. Competition is coming from below, not above. Reliance Consumer went from nothing to ₹22,000 crore of gross revenue in FY26 with Campa at ₹4,700 crore and 3 million outlets23 — playing an explicit price-disruption game. AWL Agri is at ₹74,000 crore and targeting ₹1 lakh crore by 203025. Regional challengers are taking share in every category you are in. Your defence is not price; it is brand trust, product superiority and innovation velocity.
  13. The organisation needs a different operating system, not a different strategy. Your strategy is broadly right. What is missing is speed: a portfolio-level capital allocation discipline that starves losers, an innovation engine that goes beyond 4.5% of sales, a channel-native commercial team, and a talent bar in the growth businesses that matches the ambition. Most of Part Four of this document is about that.
The one-sentence version

Tata Consumer is one of the few Indian FMCG companies with a genuine right to win across the entire pantry — and it is currently converting that right into 9% returns; the plan is to concentrate capital behind six or seven categories where you can be number one or two, exit or fix everything else, and buy your way into salty snacks and value-added dairy while the windows are still open.

And the one thing to take from the new analysis: the target is ₹42,500 crore at 16% CAGR, measured against Nestlé rather than against ITC’s non-food revenue — but on current assumptions you still finish ₹800 crore behind, so the whole plan reduces to moving the growth portfolio from 28% to 32%, and to being honest that statutory returns reach 13% rather than 18% because acquisitions are ROCE-dilutive by construction.

The five thrusts

  1. Rebalance core to growth — growth businesses from 36% to 60% of India by FY31, via capital allocation rather than exhortation.
  2. Make capital productivity the organising discipline — cost programme at 2.5% of sales, dividend payout from 64% to ~35%, one divestment completed by FY28.
  3. Convert nominal reach into effective reach — 4.5 mn outlets means little when Tata Water Plus is properly distributed in 40–50% of the country.
  4. Build one health and protein platform — ring-fenced, one leader, one P&L, ₹5,000 cr by FY31.
  5. Enter salty snacks within 24 months — buy regional at ≤3.5× revenue and nationalise it; never pay for reach you already own.

The three things most likely to go wrong

  • Quick commerce quietly costs 100bps. It pays roughly nine points less contribution than general trade. Going from 4% to 15% of the business works against the margin target every year.
  • M&A is ROCE-dilutive by construction, for a decade. An asset at 3.5× revenue would need a 63% EBIT margin to earn 18% on that capital. ₹15,000 crore deployed earns 5.0% in FY31 and does not clear an 11% cost of capital until FY34–35. Approve it as a category position with a long payback, or not at all.
  • Integration capacity, not capital, is the binding constraint. Capital Foods and Organic India remain mid-integration eighteen months on. Do not restructure and acquire in the same year.

Run this plan with no acquisitions at all and FY31 lands at roughly ₹31,500 crore — a 9.2% CAGR, and a distant second by revenue. But with no goodwill, no integration drag, no snacks mix dilution and no dividend cut, statutory ROCE reaches 16.6–18% against 13.3% in the base case, and economic profit is ₹1,470–1,824 crore against ₹1,437 crore for the stretch case that hits the revenue target.

What the executive committee is being asked to decide

Adopt the four numbers in order — economic profit first, revenue as its consequence. Publish Treasury's cost of capital so the board can tell value creation from value destruction. Price the no-acquisition counterfactual before approving the acquisition. Fund in a blend weighted to paper, not by ruling equity out. Split the returns metric by who set the price — ex-goodwill for operators, full-goodwill ROIC for the approvers, measured four to five years after close. Restructure in year one and acquire in year two, not both at once. And treat 32% growth-portfolio CAGR — not the acquisition budget — as the number the whole plan turns on, because it is the only lever that raises rank and returns together.

The shape of the market

Food and beverages is the largest slice of Indian FMCG — roughly half of it by value, and disproportionately more of the volume. The food processing industry alone was around $354 billion in FY24 and is projected near $535 billion by FY26, though most of that is unbranded and unpackaged. The strategic opportunity in India has always been the same one: the conversion of unbranded to branded. It is why salt worked, why atta worked for ITC, why spices are working for you now, and why it will work again in dairy, snacks and protein.

Three numbers frame everything you do:

  • Penetration is nearly complete; frequency and price are not. Nestlé reaches 6.2 million outlets and 45,300 villages19; ITC reaches 7 million outlets and 280 million households; you reach 4.5 million outlets and 290 million households7. Growth no longer comes from finding new buyers — it comes from selling them more, more often, at a higher price point.
  • Premiumisation is real and it is rural too. Rural India now accounts for 51% of premium volume and 42% of super-premium sales12. The old mental model of "rural = value packs, urban = premium" is obsolete and will mislead your team.
  • New product success is brutally rare. Only about 4% of new FMCG launches in India achieved even 1% market penetration. Your innovation-to-sales ratio of 4.5% is genuinely good; the industry norm is a graveyard.

1. Demand has turned, helped by policy

After eight quarters of weak volumes, the sector has re-accelerated: Q1 FY27 revenue growth of roughly 15% across the listed set13, with volume recovery visible across large, mid and small caps. Two policy actions did most of the work — the income tax relief in the FY26 budget and GST 2.0, which from 22 September 2025 collapsed the rate structure31 into 5% and 18% slabs and cut tax on a wide set of daily essentials. Consumer companies expect the GST benefit to keep supporting demand for several more quarters32.

Implication: this is a window, not a new normal. Cyclical tailwinds are the cheapest time to gain share, because competitors mistake market growth for their own performance. Push hard on distribution and A&P now.

Update · October 2026 — the window is narrowing

NielsenIQ's April–June 2026 read is the first hard evidence that the window is closing. All-India FMCG value grew 0.8% on a 2% volume decline, and 68% of categories lost volume51. The listed set did not see it: Nestlé +25%, Marico +23%, Godrej Consumer +18%, Tata Consumer +12%. Organised players are taking share of a pie that has stopped growing. The implication above still holds, and it is more urgent: share gained now is gained from weaker competitors, not from the market.

2. Rural is outperforming urban — a reversal

Rural markets have grown ahead of urban for three consecutive quarters. Rural also over-indexes on premium: 51% of premium volume, 42% of super-premium sales. Meanwhile urban consumption is under pressure from household debt at roughly 42% of GDP and elevated cost of living in metros.

Implication: the growth portfolio — Sampann, RTD, Soulfull — has been built for urban and modern-trade first. Rural is a large, under-served opportunity for exactly these brands, and it needs different pack-price architecture, not just wider distribution.

Update · October 2026 — rural has turned

Rural led urban for eight consecutive quarters to December 2025. In April–June 2026 rural volumes fell 5% and rural value fell 2.9%, with traditional trade dragging the decline51. One quarter is not a trend, but a deficit monsoon makes the next two quarters the test. The rural opportunity is real over the plan period. In FY27, plan rural pack-price moves on the assumption that the consumer is squeezed.

3. K-shaped consumption

The middle is hollowing. Premium and super-premium are growing fastest; mass-value is growing on volume with almost no pricing power; mid-market is being squeezed from both ends. HUL's Q1 FY27 is the clearest illustration — premium hair and skin care in double digits, personal care up only 4% and almost entirely on price.

Implication: a barbell portfolio beats a middle-of-the-road one. Every category team should be able to name their premium play and their mass-entry play separately.

4. Health, wellness and the protein wave

The self-directed health consumer is the defining consumer of this decade — reading labels, tracking macros, buying on ingredient claims rather than brand heritage alone. India's protein market is compounding at roughly 17.7%. Sugar reduction, sodium reduction, millets, fortification and "clean label" have moved from niche to mainstream expectation.

Implication: this is Tata Consumer's most natural adjacency and it is currently under-exploited. Lo-Sodium salt, Hi-Protein Soulfull and Sampann protein snacking are the right instincts; they are not yet a coherent, branded health platform.

5. Channel fragmentation and the quick-commerce shock

Covered in depth in section 05. The short version: quick commerce is compounding at 70–80% and already commands 70–75% of e-grocery from 35% in 2022, while general trade has fallen to 56% of your business. Traditional trade is under real economic stress, with distributor bodies escalating to government over channel price parity.

Implication: your commercial organisation, pack architecture, trade terms and supply chain were all built for a channel structure that no longer exists.

6. Challenger insurgency — from below and from scale

Two different threats, often confused. Regional challengers — Balaji, Wagh Bakri, Society, hundreds of local players — win on freshness, price and local taste, and are structurally advantaged in a high-freight, high-GST-compliance world. Deep-pocketed entrants — Reliance Consumer above all — buy share with price and distribution scale that no P&L-constrained competitor can match.

Implication: you cannot out-price either. You can out-innovate, out-quality and out-distribute. Your CEO commentary already says this; the organisation needs to believe it under pressure.

7. Input cost and currency volatility as a permanent condition

Tea procurement inflation is running at 7–10%. Palm oil and crude-linked packaging costs are elevated. Robusta coffee fell 26% year-on-year, hitting the non-branded business hard. Freight, fuel and FX are all moving.

Implication: pricing agility is a capability, not an event. The companies that win margin cycles are those that can take a ₹2 price increase in salt or a selective tea hike within weeks of a cost signal — and reverse it as fast when costs deflate.

The four levers, in order of power

  1. Mix, not price
    Moving a point of revenue from a 20% gross margin staple to a 45% gross margin branded food is worth more than any pricing action you can safely take. Nestlé's 26% EBITDA margin is a mix outcome. Your 13.9% is also a mix outcome. Mix is the lever; everything else is refinement.
  2. Gross-margin-accretive innovation
    New products should carry margins above the existing portfolio average, not below. The trap in Indian FMCG is launching at price parity to buy trial and never recovering. Capital Foods and Organic India run at close to 50% gross margin — that is the standard for the growth portfolio.
  3. Cost-to-serve, not cost-of-goods
    Nestlé's operating cost savings reached 2.6% of sales in 2025 against a 1.9% four-year average, and that alone contributed heavily to a 72% EBITDA increase. The savings came from network design, complexity reduction and productivity — not from procurement squeezing.
  4. Working capital and capital intensity
    This is where ROCE lives. Every rupee of goodwill, inventory day and receivable day is capital employed. A company with 15% EBITDA and 45 days of working capital can out-return a company with 20% EBITDA and 90 days.
Where Tata Consumer sits

13.9% consolidated EBITDA margin in FY26 versus HUL at 23.6%17, Nestlé India near 26%, Britannia at roughly 18.5%. Roughly half of that gap is genuine mix — you carry a large low-margin non-branded and commodity-adjacent business, and salt and tea are structurally lower-margin than biscuits or noodles. The other half is addressable: complexity, sub-scale growth brands, and a cost base built for a smaller, simpler company.

The ROCE equation you should manage to

Return on capital employed decomposes cleanly into three things a CEO can act on:

ROCE = EBIT margin × (Revenue ÷ Capital Employed)

At roughly ₹25,000 crore of capital employed and around ₹2,300 crore of EBIT, you are running a capital turn of about 0.8× and an EBIT margin near 11.4%. Peers with comparable revenue run capital turns of 1.5–3× because they carry far less goodwill and far lighter fixed assets. Your ROCE problem is at least as much a balance sheet problem as a margin problem — and no amount of revenue growth fixes it unless the incremental revenue comes at high asset turns.

Six shifts in how India buys food

The self-directed health consumer

The single largest behavioural change of this decade. Consumers are making health decisions without a doctor, an expert or a brand telling them what to do — reading labels, checking protein grams per serve, avoiding palm oil, counting added sugar. They trust ingredient lists more than advertising claims.

This consumer is not fringe. She is urban, aspirational, aged 25–45, increasingly rural-adjacent, and she disproportionately buys online where the label is fully visible before purchase. She is precisely the consumer for whom Tata Consumer's brand trust is worth the most — and precisely the consumer your core tea and salt portfolio was not designed for.

Brief: every category needs a "clean and functional" tier, with the health claim on the front of pack and substantiated on the back. Subtraction claims (lower sodium, no added sugar) are table stakes; addition claims (protein, fibre, micronutrients, millets) are the growth engine.

Premiumisation is now a rural phenomenon

Rural India delivers 51% of premium volume and 42% of super-premium sales. The driver is not rural incomes converging with urban — it is that a rural household buying its first branded product often buys the aspirational one, because the purchase is an identity statement. This has been true in tea for decades; it is now true across the pantry.

Brief: stop treating rural as a value-pack market. Take premium variants to rural in small packs at premium per-gram pricing, not down-specified versions.

Convenience is compressing the cooking occasion

Dual-income households, smaller family units, and a generation that did not learn to cook from a mother-in-law. The winners are products that remove a step: ready-to-cook pastes, cup formats, one-minute breakfast, pre-mixed masalas. Capital Foods' Manchurian and Schezwan cup noodles are exactly this thesis; Sampann's ready-to-cook range is the same idea in Indian cuisine.

Brief: the strategic question in Indian foods is no longer "what do people eat" but "how many steps will they tolerate." Build for two-step and one-step.

Trust has become a purchasing filter, and it favours you

Adulteration scares, pesticide residue stories, and mis-selling in nutrition have made provenance a genuine tiebreaker. In categories where the consumer cannot verify quality — spices, pulses, honey, ghee, protein — brand trust converts directly to price premium. The Tata name is one of a very small set of Indian brands that carries this in every state and every income bracket.

Brief: trust is your most valuable and most under-monetised asset. It should be the reason you enter categories with the worst adulteration reputations, not the safest ones.

The discovery layer has moved online, even for offline purchases

With over a billion internet subscribers and average smartphone use of roughly five hours a day, product discovery for packaged foods happens on Instagram, YouTube and quick-commerce search bars — then converts wherever is convenient. E-commerce penetration is around 6% nationally but roughly 18% in the top eight cities and 21% in southern metros.

Brief: your share of search on Blinkit and Zepto is now a leading indicator of your share of shelf in general trade, because the platforms shape what people ask for. Treat platform search share as a tracked marketing KPI.

Price sensitivity has not gone away — it has become sharper

Household debt at roughly 42% of GDP and urban cost-of-living pressure mean consumers trade down within a brand rather than away from it: smaller packs, fewer units, waiting for a promotion. Campa's success at 10% market share came from an explicit price-disruption play that gave consumers permission to switch.

Brief: price-pack architecture is a strategic weapon, not a packaging decision. Every brand needs a defensible entry pack that protects the franchise when the consumer is squeezed.

The quick-commerce deep dive

Quick commerce is the most consequential channel development in Indian FMCG since modern trade arrived, and it is misunderstood inside most FMCG companies as "another e-commerce account." It is not. Five things make it structurally different:

  1. The assortment is brutally small
    A dark store carries a few thousand SKUs against a supermarket's tens of thousands. Getting listed is a zero-sum fight, and de-listing is instant and data-driven. Your long tail dies here — which is a gift if you use it to force portfolio discipline.
  2. Search rank is the shelf
    Position in platform search results is the equivalent of eye-level shelf placement, and it is bought as well as earned. Advertising spend on Blinkit, Zepto and Instamart is heading toward roughly ₹4,900 crore in 2026. This is a new, permanent, and rising line in your marketing budget that did not exist five years ago.
  3. Pack architecture must be rebuilt
    Quick commerce is an immediacy channel — the consumer wants one thing now. Large family packs under-index; single-serve, mid-size and "top-up" packs over-index. Companies that ship their general trade pack ladder to q-commerce lose.
  4. It cannibalises modern trade first, then general trade
    Modern trade is already declining in relative terms as q-commerce grows. General trade feels it later but harder: reports of around 200,000 kirana closures and 25–30% sales declines in affected urban catchments.
  5. It has created a live channel-conflict problem
    The All India Consumer Products Distributors Federation has formally demanded price parity across channels and escalated to government over platform IPOs. Your distributors see promotional pricing on Blinkit that undercuts what they are charged. This is now a governance issue, not a trade-terms issue.
The measurement blind spot

Your own management has stopped disclosing Nielsen-based market share because general trade is only 56% of the business and conventional retail audits no longer represent the market. That is analytically honest — but it means the company currently has no single, trusted, board-level view of competitive share. Building a blended share metric that combines retail audit, platform data and consumer panel is one of the highest-return analytics projects available to you, and it should be commissioned in your first month.

What good channel strategy looks like from here

The industry's answer — visible in what ITC, Nestlé, Dabur, Parle, Coca-Cola and Tata Consumer are all doing simultaneously — is a re-embrace of the kirana alongside q-commerce investment: raising trade margins on select brands, shortening replenishment cycles, deploying predictive demand forecasting for distributors, and expanding direct coverage. ITC's UNNATI B2B platform now engages over 800,000 outlets; its digitally-enabled and modern trade channels are 34% of FMCG sales with q-commerce up 57% in FY26.

The correct stance is not to choose. It is to run differentiated portfolios per channel — different pack sizes, different price points, different assortment depth — so that the channels compete for different occasions rather than for the same transaction at different prices. That is the only durable answer to channel conflict, and it is an execution problem, not a policy problem.

The number that should change your channel strategy

Quick commerce delivers roughly nine points less contribution than general trade on the same product. If quick commerce goes from around 4% of your business to 15% by FY29 — which is the mid-case, not the bull case — that mix shift alone costs roughly 100 basis points of company contribution margin, before a single input cost moves. You are being asked to expand margin by 400bps while the channel mix is quietly working 100bps against you.

Why the D2C column is not an argument against D2C

D2C shows the worst contribution on this table and it is still worth building — because the table shows a first order. Customer acquisition is a one-time cost amortised across a lifetime of repeat purchases. On a repeat order with no acquisition cost, D2C contribution rises from 13 to roughly 27 — the best of any channel. The entire D2C business case is therefore a repeat-rate case, not a revenue case.

That has a hard managerial consequence: the only D2C metric that matters at board level is the share of revenue from repeat cohorts. If your team reports D2C growth without reporting repeat rate, they are reporting a number that can be bought with discounts. Set the bar at 55% of D2C revenue from repeat purchasers before the channel is judged viable, and be willing to shut it if it does not clear.

The pack-price architecture that makes channel conflict disappear

Channel conflict is almost always self-inflicted. It happens when the same SKU, at the same grammage, is available in two channels at different effective prices — at which point the kirana owner sees a dark store undercutting them on the exact pack they stock, and the distributor federation writes to the government. The fix is not a pricing policy. It is a grammage architecture in which no two channels carry a directly comparable pack.

The threat model: Reliance is not optimising for profit in this phase. It is buying share with price and shelf, funded from elsewhere in the group, with the intent of building a top-tier consumer business by 2030. Any category it enters sees immediate price compression.

Your defence: product superiority, brand trust and innovation velocity — exactly what your own management has said. Practically, that means never matching a Reliance price cut in a category where you have a genuine quality advantage, and instead widening the quality gap and communicating it. ₹74,000 crore in FY26 with a food segment of ₹6,400 crore and Fortune alone above ₹30,000 crore. Reach of 2.6 million outlets and 60,000 rural towns; Fortune in one of every three Indian households. Targeting ₹1 lakh crore total revenue and doubled food revenue by 2030, with ROCE above 20%.

Why they matter: AWL is executing exactly the "oil-to-foods" adjacency play that ITC executed with atta, and it competes head-on with Sampann in rice, atta, besan and soya. Its distribution in rural staples is deeper than yours.

Britannia, Marico and Dabur — the mid-caps

Britannia at ₹19,151 crore and 7.1% growth is the cautionary tale: category dominance in biscuits without a second act, defending an 18.5% margin while growth stalls. Marico is the opposite and the most instructive: ₹13,611 crore with 26% growth — its fastest in 14 years — driven by 28% India growth and successful diversification from coconut oil into foods and digital-first brands, guiding to ₹15,000 crore and 150bps of margin expansion. Dabur is rebuilding, with modest growth and 60bps of margin recovery.

The lesson from Marico: a mid-cap with a commodity-linked core successfully built a high-growth foods and premium portfolio around it, and the market rewarded it. That is a close analogue of the transition Tata Consumer is attempting, and it is proof the transition is possible.

Amul and the cooperative reality

₹1 lakh crore of brand turnover in FY26, up 11%, with GCMMF at ₹73,450 crore; 3.6 million farmers, 31 million litres collected daily, 1,200+ product packs, 24 billion packs distributed. Amul does not maximise profit — it maximises farmer realisation — which makes it structurally impossible to out-compete on price in dairy.

Implication for you: any dairy entry must be in value-added, premium or convenience dairy where Amul's cost advantage matters less and brand and innovation matter more. Never in liquid milk or commodity butter.

What It Takes to Win

Across every winner in this industry, the same seven capabilities recur. This is the scorecard to hold your executive team against — and Tata Consumer's honest grade on each.

Read the full chapterCollapse chapter
  1. Category leadership, not category presence Partial
    The number one or two brand in a category earns three to five times the economic profit of the number four. Nestlé is number one in almost everything it sells. Britannia is number one in biscuits. You are number one in salt and roughly number one in tea — and outside the top three in most of the growth portfolio. The discipline: be number one or two, or have a credible three-year path to it, or exit.
  2. Distribution depth measured in effective reach, not outlets Weak
    Everyone quotes outlet numbers. The number that matters is effective reach: outlets that stock your specific SKU, in stock, at the right price. Tata Water Plus is available in 75% of the country but properly distributed in only 40–50%. That gap, repeated across the growth portfolio, is the largest single revenue leak in the company.
  3. Innovation velocity with a commercial filter Strong
    80 launches in FY26, innovation-to-sales at 4.5%7, innovation revenue up seven-fold since FY21. This is genuinely best-in-class in India. The risk is the opposite one: proliferation without pruning. The test of a mature innovation engine is how many SKUs it kills each year, not how many it launches.
  4. Brand investment at scale, concentrated Partial
    A&P at 6.1% of revenue in Q1 FY27 is respectable but spread across too many brands. The winners spend disproportionately on fewer, bigger brands. Nestlé built Maggi and Nescafé into franchises by never under-investing in them. The discipline: no brand gets A&P unless it can be a ₹1,000 crore brand.
  5. Cost and capital productivity as a permanent programme Weak
    Nestlé's savings run at 2.6% of sales as a structural programme, not a cost-cutting exercise. Your ROCE of 9.2% against a ~60× multiple says this capability does not yet exist at the required intensity. This is the biggest gap in the company.
  6. Channel-native commercial capability Building
    Running general trade, modern trade, quick commerce and D2C as genuinely different businesses with different portfolios, terms, teams and P&Ls. Most Indian FMCG companies still run one commercial organisation with an e-commerce team bolted on. The ones that separate them properly are winning.
  7. Talent density in the growth businesses Partial
    The candid admission in your own Q1 FY27 call — trouble finding distributors and distributor salesmen for Capital Foods and Organic India — is a talent and capability constraint, not a market constraint. Growth businesses need your best people, not your available ones.
The synthesis

Tata Consumer is strong where the industry rewards creativity (innovation, brand trust, portfolio breadth) and weak where it rewards discipline (capital productivity, effective distribution, focus). Every high-performing FMCG company in India is strong on both. The next three years are about installing the discipline without losing the creativity — which is primarily an operating-system and talent problem, not a strategy problem.

Why this argues against managing to a relative target at all

The ₹42,500 crore target swings by roughly ₹4,000 crore — and the required CAGR by three full points — on two points of a competitor's growth rate that you neither control nor forecast well. A target that unstable cannot be the number an organisation is managed to: in the year Nestlé outperforms, every operating team is missing a goal for reasons entirely outside the business.

So separate the two uses. The number the company manages to is absolute: ₹42,500 crore by FY31, at a spread above the cost of capital. That is fixed, it is in the scorecard, and it does not move. Relative rank is reported, not targeted — reviewed annually against where Nestlé has actually got to, with the bar restated rather than the plan rewritten. If Nestlé compounds at 15%, the honest board conversation is that leadership by revenue has moved out of reach in this plan period, and the right response is to say so, not to add ₹6,000 crore of acquisitions at multiples that destroy value.

Why this target is ₹42,500 crore and not ₹46,000

An earlier reading of this comparison set the bar at ₹46,000 crore by treating ITC's ₹40,790 crore of total FMCG revenue as the number to beat. It is not the right comparison: that figure includes personal care, agarbatti, matches, education and stationery. ITC Foods — the business you actually compete with — is a little over ₹20,000 crore20 and reaches roughly ₹35,250 crore by FY31.

Measured on a like-for-like branded food and beverage basis, the competitor that defines leadership is Nestlé India at about ₹40,660 crore. Clearing that with enough margin to be beyond forecast error means ₹42,500 crore — a 16% CAGR. That is not a softened ambition; it is the same ambition measured against the right company, and it removes roughly ₹4,000 crore of acquisitions from the funding requirement. Section 17 builds the full bridge to this number.

The gap, stated precisely

Roughly ₹5,100 crore of FY31 revenue separates the current 13% trajectory from clear leadership. That is the number your strategy has to close — and closing it at 13.9% EBITDA and 9% ROCE would be a failure, not a success. Section 17 shows that even the full plan, competently executed, closes only about four-fifths of it: the base case lands at ₹39,800 crore, behind Nestlé. Leadership depends on the growth portfolio compounding at 32% rather than 28%.

The objective is economic profit; revenue rank is a consequence

Section 17 shows that pursuing ₹42,500 crore drives statutory ROCE to 13–14% rather than 18%. That is usually read as a measurement problem — two metrics that need reconciling. It is not. It is the plan telling you that the two goals point in opposite directions, because the acquisitions that deliver the revenue add capital at 3.5× revenue while the returns target needs a capital turn above 1.2×. When a plan's own arithmetic says two objectives conflict, the answer is to rank them, not to publish both and hope.

So the primary objective is economic profit: (ROCE − WACC) × capital employed. It is the only measure that cannot be gamed by growth, because every rupee of acquired revenue arrives with the capital that bought it. Revenue leadership is the expected by-product of getting it right — not the thing being maximised.

Can you fund it?

Honest answer: partially, and only with a capital allocation change.

  • Retained cash. Free cash flow was ₹2,016 crore in FY26 and will grow with the business. At the current 64% dividend payout, retention is roughly ₹550 crore a year.   • Debt capacity. With ₹2,820 crore of borrowings against ₹2,815 crore1 of EBITDA, leverage is negligible. Taking net debt to a still-conservative 1.5× EBITDA provides ₹5,000–6,000 crore of additional firepower, rising as EBITDA grows.
  • Capital release. A partial or full exit from non-branded, and a strategic review of the UK, could release meaningful capital and simultaneously improve ROCE — the rare move that helps both sides of the equation.

You cannot buy your way to number one. What "harvest" must not be allowed to mean, in tea specifically

Tea sits in the harvest bucket for the right reason — its category compounds at under 3% and will not reward growth investment. But two distinctions have to travel with that classification, or the bucket does damage.

First, there is almost no capital here to release. Tea's capital is brand and working capital, not fixed assets. Unlike the non-branded plantations and extraction business — where a divestment genuinely frees ₹2,800 crore and lifts ROCE — harvesting tea produces cash flow, not capital release. Any funding plan that counts on tea as a source of capital is counting something that does not exist.

Second, and more important: tea holds the shelf. The 4.5 million outlets that section 16 calls your most under-used asset are not reached in the abstract. They are reached because the distributor's route, the retailer's relationship and the shelf facings are paid for by the volume that tea and salt move through them. Tea is the carrier that puts Sampann, Soulfull and the rest of the growth portfolio in front of a kirana owner. Under-fund it — cut A&P, thin the servicing frequency, let share slip to Wagh Bakri and the regional packers — and you do not merely lose tea profit. You degrade the distribution asset that the entire growth plan is riding on, at exactly the moment the plan needs it to carry more.

So the mandate is: maximise cash and margin, hold share and shelf, release no capital because there is none to release. Squeezing tea to fund snacks would be borrowing from the distribution system to buy a business that section 15 shows cannot use it.

That is a real opportunity, but it demands cold-chain capability you do not currently have, which makes it a second-wave move rather than a first one.

The trap in the "equity is expensive" argument

Put the two branches side by side and notice that both of them favour issuing:

If 62.9× is justified — the market is right that this is a compounder — then equity is exceptionally cheap currency and you should use it to buy assets trading at 39×.

If 62.9× is not justified — which is what conclusion 1 implies, since a business earning 9.2% against an 11% cost of capital does not obviously deserve a ~60× multiple — then issuing overvalued paper transfers value from incoming shareholders to your existing ones. That is an argument to issue more, not less.

Equity is the wrong currency in exactly one case: when the stock is undervalued. Nobody anywhere in this document argues that it is. The threat register in section 11 makes the opposite case, warning that any growth disappointment de-rates the stock sharply — which is a reason to issue while the multiple holds, not a reason to rule it out.

One caveat that must travel with this, or it becomes an excuse

Funding the ₹15,000 crore entirely in paper is EPS-accretive by about 9% on day one — earnings rise roughly 25% against 14.4% more shares — while the same transaction earns 2.6% on invested capital in its first year. Both are true, and the gap between them is precisely how overpriced acquisitions get approved: the accretion is immediate and visible, the returns shortfall arrives years later in someone else's tenure.

So hold the two apart. Paper is the right currency; it does not make a bad price a good one. The 3.5× walk-away in section 15 and the full-goodwill ROIC measure in section 19 are what keep the currency argument from becoming a licence to overpay. Fund in equity, and hold the price discipline exactly where it was.

The recommendation: a blend, weighted to paper. Roughly ₹6,000–8,000 crore of equity, ₹4,000–5,000 crore of debt, and the balance from retained cash — which preserves the dividend closer to 45–50% than 35%, keeps the balance sheet intact for the price-war scenario, and leaves the retention pool free for the organic growth portfolio that section 17 identifies as the highest-return use of every marginal rupee. Put all three options in front of the board with the arithmetic attached, rather than a single recommendation with equity ruled out in a subordinate clause.

What this is, and what it is not

The arbitrage, stated plainly

A regional snacks leader is valued on the geography it can reach. You are buying it for the geography you can reach. A ₹1,200 crore business dominant in two states, bought at 3× revenue, becomes a ₹4,000–5,000 crore business on your distribution within four years — which means you paid roughly 0.8× the revenue you will actually own. Buying the national number two means paying full price for a distribution network that duplicates your own. Pay for taste and plants. Never pay for reach.

Your right to win here is not the one the adjacency argument claims

The standard case for this entry is adjacency: same consumer, same pantry occasion, same distribution, same trust. Three of those four are true and one of them is doing work it cannot support — and getting this wrong is how acquirers overpay.

Distribution is not the binding constraint in salty snacks. If it were, the category would be led by whoever has the widest reach, and it is not. Haldiram holds roughly 36.6% on taste authority built over decades. Balaji owns Gujarat — a single state — with a recipe and a fry-line cost position that national players with far greater reach have failed to dislodge28. PepsiCo, with world-class distribution and unlimited capital, holds 3.3%. ITC, with 7 million outlets, holds 3.1%. Reach is demonstrably not what wins this category.

What binds is a different list: taste authority, regional recipe credibility, and fry-line scale — the ability to make a product a consumer prefers, in a blend that is locally right, at a conversion cost the regional incumbent has spent twenty years grinding down.

Which sharpens the rule rather than softening it

Distribution is the thing you already have. The scarce inputs — taste, recipe credibility, fry lines inside the freshness radius — are precisely what you would be paying for. That is the same conclusion as "never pay for reach you already own", stated more usefully: you are not buying a distribution multiplier, you are buying the three capabilities that reach cannot substitute for.

It also explains the price. Regional snacks assets are expensive because the scarce input is scarce — not because sellers are unreasonable. A ₹1,200 crore two-state business commands 3× revenue for the same reason you want it: the taste authority took twenty years and cannot be bought anywhere else. Underwrite that honestly and the multiple makes sense. Underwrite it as "our distribution will multiply their revenue" and you will pay the regional premium and a control premium for an asset whose real value you have mis-specified — and you will fire the wrong people after close, because you will believe the commercial team is what you bought.

    At 4× and above, the arbitrage that justifies this whole route disappears — you are paying national multiples for a regional asset and the returns depend on flawless national expansion. Your own recent history says do not underwrite flawless.

Ten points of post-close CAGR is worth ₹1,853 crore of FY31 revenue, and it more than triples the shortfall against Nestlé — from ₹818 crore to ₹2,670 crore. That is a larger swing than any operating lever in the margin walk, and it is assumed rather than argued.

The returns walk — and the contradiction at the centre of the plan

ROCE is the number the document rightly calls central. It is also the number where the plan is internally inconsistent, and the inconsistency is arithmetic rather than a matter of opinion.

An acquisition made at 3.5× revenue enters your capital base at 3.5× revenue. For that asset to earn an 18% return on the capital you paid, it would need an EBIT margin of 3.5 × 18% = 63%. No food business earns that. At a realistic 15% EBIT margin, an asset bought at 3.5× revenue earns roughly 4.3% ROCE in year one, and only reaches 18% once its revenue has roughly quadrupled on the same invested capital.

You cannot have both — so measure both

An 18% statutory ROCE by FY31 and a ₹42,500 crore revenue target are mutually exclusive, because the acquisitions that deliver the revenue add capital at 3.5× while your target capital turn is 1.2×. The resolution is not to abandon either goal. It is to run two returns metrics and be explicit about which is which:

Statutory ROCE — for the board and the market. Target 13–14% by FY31, against an ~11% cost of capital, and 18% by FY34. Say this publicly, with the acquisition arithmetic attached, before an analyst says it for you.

Operating ROCE, excluding acquisition goodwill — for the operators, and only for them. Target 18% by FY29. This is the return an operating team actually controls, and holding someone to a number polluted by a deal multiple they did not set is both unfair and ineffective.

Full-goodwill ROIC, measured four to five years after close — for whoever approved the deal: CEO, CFO and Corporate Development, with long-term vesting attached to it. This is the half that is usually missing, and its absence is what makes an ex-goodwill metric dangerous.

Plus an annual goodwill return test — every acquisition measured against the hurdle and horizon in section 14, with impairment taken honestly and promptly when it misses. Section 19 sets out how the split is applied to individual packages.

Organisation Design & Decision Rights

Eleven playbooks describe what each leader should do. This section describes the structure that lets them do it — because the strategy in Part Three cannot be executed by the organisation that exists today, and the reason is structural rather than a matter of effort.

The mismatch, stated plainly

Tata Consumer is organised primarily by geography and segment — India Foods, India Beverages, International, Non-branded. That structure was correct for the merger. It optimises for distribution leverage and it made sense when the company was a tea-and-salt business extending its reach.

But the strategy in Part Three is a category and channel strategy. It asks you to be number one or two in eight categories, to run four channels with genuinely different economics, and to grow a ring-fenced growth portfolio at 32%. A geography-led structure cannot deliver that, for a specific reason: nobody owns a category end-to-end. Gross margin sits with supply chain, A&P sits with marketing, pricing sits with sales, and the person whose name is on the category's growth target controls none of the three.

The diagnostic question

Ask your team: "Who is accountable for the gross margin of Tata Sampann spices?" If more than one name comes back, or if the honest answer is "it's shared", you have found the reason the growth portfolio compounds at 28% rather than 32%. Categories do not have owners in this company. They have sponsors.

Four structural changes

  1. Category general managers with a real P&L
    Create GM roles for the eight categories you intend to lead — salt, spices and condiments, snacks, RTD, coffee, health and protein, Indian-Chinese, tea — each owning revenue, gross margin, A&P and innovation for their category, and each carrying a personal target for a number one or two position. Geography structures optimise for reach; category structures optimise for share and margin, and share and margin are what the plan needs. The Presidents remain, but as owners of the go-to-market engine that all categories draw on rather than as owners of the categories themselves.

    The hard part: this takes power from two of your most senior people. Do it in one move, publicly, with the logic explained — a phased version will be fought for eighteen months and arrive diluted.
  2. One Chief Commercial Officer, four channel P&Ls beneath
    General trade, modern trade, quick commerce and e-commerce, and D2C — each with its own head, own portfolio, own trade terms, own team and its own P&L down to contribution as set out in section 06. Today, like most Indian FMCG companies, you run a single commercial organisation with an e-commerce team attached to the side. The channels have different economics, different assortment logic, different servicing frequencies and different customers; running them as one organisation guarantees that the largest channel's habits govern all four.

    The test that it is real: each channel head can show you their contribution margin without a finance project. Most cannot today.
  3. Ring-fence the health and protein platform
    Section 16 recommends creating the platform. Recommend further: ring-fence it. Its own P&L, its own innovation pipeline, its own talent bar, reporting directly to you, and — critically — exempt from the corporate allocation and the quarterly margin conversation for three years. Growth businesses embedded inside large-company P&Ls die a predictable death: they get taxed by allocations they did not incur and starved in any quarter where the group needs margin. The ring-fence is what makes the 32% CAGR possible.

    With a hard edge: ring-fenced does not mean unaccountable. It means measured on three-year revenue, share and repeat rate instead of quarterly EBITDA — and closed decisively if it misses the eighteen-month hurdle.
  4. A permanent Integration & Value Capture office
    Section 14 identifies integration capacity, not capital, as your binding constraint — and the evidence is that Capital Foods and Organic India remain mid-integration eighteen months on. A binding constraint deserves a standing function, not a project team assembled per deal and dissolved after close. Staff it permanently under the CFO, give it the synergy tracking, the GTM rebuild and the systems migration, and give its head a vote on whether the organisation can absorb the next transaction.

    Why under the CFO: integration value is measured in cash and capital, and the office needs to be able to tell a business leader that a synergy has not been delivered.
Why this ordering matters more than it looks

An ex-goodwill metric applied to everyone converts a disclosure discipline into a licence. It says, in effect, that the purchase price is somebody else's problem — and in practice that means nobody's, because the deal team's incentive has usually vested by the time the answer is in. Pairing it with a full-goodwill measure on the approvers, assessed on the horizon the arithmetic actually requires, is what keeps the two-metric system honest. Without the second half, section 17's recommendation should not be adopted at all.

The single most important row

Kill decisions must default to kill. In almost every FMCG company, discontinuing a product requires someone to actively advocate for its death — which nobody ever does, because the person best placed to know launched it. Invert it: every launch carries a defined twelve-month hurdle set at launch, and missing it discontinues the product automatically unless the category GM formally contests in writing. This one change does more to prevent portfolio bloat than any rationalisation programme, because it works continuously instead of once every four years.

Talent and reward: four changes that make the structure work

  1. Split the returns metric by who set the price — not by seniority
    Section 17 recommends measuring management on ROCE excluding acquisition goodwill. Applied to everyone, that recommendation is actively dangerous: an organisation rewarded on ex-goodwill returns is rewarded for paying any price for anything. The person who negotiates the multiple is insulated from it, and the multiple is the second-largest lever in the entire plan. The split has to run along the line of who controlled the price:

    Operators — category GMs, the acquired leadership, everyone running the business after close — are measured on operating ROCE excluding acquisition goodwill. They inherited a price they did not set; holding them to it is unfair and, worse, ineffective, because it is not a number their decisions move.

    Deal approvers — the CEO, the CFO and Corporate Development — are measured on full-goodwill ROIC, including every rupee of the purchase price, assessed four to five years after close, with a meaningful share of long-term vesting attached to that assessment. Four to five years is the point at which the answer is knowable: section 14 shows the return is roughly 5% at year five and does not clear the cost of capital until FY34–35, so a shorter window measures integration noise rather than whether the price was right.

    Success looks like: the people who sign the cheque carry the cheque. The people who run what it bought carry what they can control.
  2. Pay the growth businesses differently
    A leader running a ₹400 crore business growing at 40% is doing harder, riskier work than one running a ₹3,000 crore business growing at 4% — and in most FMCG reward structures the second is paid more, because pay tracks scale. Rebuild the growth-portfolio package with a lower fixed component and a materially larger long-term award tied to three-year revenue and share, not annual EBITDA. Until you do this, the scorecard target of 60% of top-100 talent in growth businesses will not be met, because your best people are rationally declining those roles.
  3. Make talent reallocation a decision, not an aspiration
    The half-yearly talent review should end with named moves, not a map. Set an explicit quota — a stated number of top-100 executives moving from core to growth businesses each half-year — and hold it as a CHRO deliverable. Aspirational talent metrics without a forcing mechanism produce a beautifully maintained spreadsheet and no movement.
  4. Treat frontline recruitment as an operations problem, not an HR one
    The distributor and distributor-salesman vacancies in Capital Foods and Organic India are the single most concrete constraint named anywhere in this document — your own management flagged them on the Q1 FY27 call. That will not be fixed by HR business partners supporting the sales team. It needs a dedicated frontline talent operation with its own funnel, its own conversion metrics and weekly reporting to the CEO until vacancies reach zero. Applying a corporate-recruitment model to a frontline-hiring problem is why the vacancies have persisted.
A warning about sequencing

Do not attempt the structural change and the salty snacks acquisition in the same twelve months. A reorganisation consumes roughly two quarters of managerial attention, and integration capacity is already your binding constraint. Restructure first, in year one; acquire in year two. A company midway through a reorganisation is the worst possible acquirer — reporting lines are unclear, the integration office has no authority, and the acquired leadership team cannot tell who they work for. Getting this order wrong is one of the more plausible ways this plan fails.

Scenario one — Reliance opens a price war in your core

Likelihood ~40%
Mechanism
Reliance Consumer applies the Campa playbook to salt or branded staples: a recognised proposition at 25–30% below your price, backed by 3 million outlets and cross-subsidised from retail and telecom. It took roughly 10% of the cola market in about three years, and it is explicitly not profit-constrained. Your salt franchise, at ~39% share and 40%+ gross margin, is the most attractive target in your portfolio precisely because it is the most profitable.
P&L impact
If you defend with an 8% realisation cut across salt, that is roughly ₹250–350 crore of EBITDA — over a tenth of the group total — for a share outcome that is uncertain at best. Modelled
Doctrine
Never match on price. Widen the quality and purity gap and communicate it relentlessly; accelerate the premium tier where price comparison is weakest; defend distribution intensity rather than price point. Accept a 2–3 point share loss in the value tier as the cost of protecting the premium tier absolutely. Salt is a trust purchase, and trust is the one thing a price entrant cannot discount into existence.
Pre-commit
Agree a published internal price floor with the board now — a stated level below which you will not follow, whatever the share consequence. Without it, the decision gets made in a bad week by whoever is most alarmed.
Trigger
Distributor margin demands rising in the affected category · a competitor entering an adjacent SKU at more than 20% below your price · value-tier volume declining while premium holds.

Scenario two — quick commerce reaches 20% of urban FMCG

Likelihood ~55%
Mechanism
Quick commerce is compounding at 70–80% and already takes 70–75% of e-grocery. At that rate it moves from roughly 4% of your business to 15% by FY29 — and it carries only 10–15% of a supermarket's assortment, so the shift is not just a margin event but a delisting event for your tail.
P&L impact
Roughly 100bps of company contribution margin on the mix shift alone, per section 06. Plus a second-order effect that is larger and less discussed: if 30% of your SKUs cannot earn a place on the two dominant platforms, the innovation pipeline loses its route to the urban consumer who tries new products first.
Doctrine
Channel-specific pack architecture so no SKU is price-comparable across channels; retail media bought against contribution rather than revenue and brought in-house rather than left to agencies; and an explicit decision about which 200 SKUs constitute your quick-commerce portfolio, taken by you rather than by a platform's category manager.
Pre-commit
Build the in-house retail media capability before you need it. The ₹4,900 crore platform ad pool is priced as marketing and behaves as trade spend; the companies that will manage it well are the ones that built the measurement capability while the spend was still small.
Trigger
Quick commerce crossing 10% of urban revenue · platform commission plus ad spend exceeding 14% of quick-commerce net revenue · more than 30% of your SKUs unlisted on the top two platforms.

Scenario three — tea inflation persists above 10% for eight quarters

Likelihood ~35%
Mechanism
Procurement inflation of 7–10% is already the reported condition, and climate volatility in Assam and the Nilgiris makes persistence more likely than reversion. Tea remains your largest single revenue line, inside a category growing under 3%, where consumers are unusually price-sensitive and regional competitors are structurally advantaged on freshness and cost.
P&L impact
At 10% procurement inflation with a 120-day pass-through lag, roughly 200–300bps of India Beverages margin, persisting until pricing catches up. Modelled The compounding damage is worse than the annual figure: each quarter of lag is margin you never recover.
Doctrine
The 60-day pricing mechanism from the beverages playbook is the whole answer and it is an operational change, not a strategic one. Pair it with forward-buying governance and an accelerated shift into value-added formats, where gross margin is a multiple of black CTC and price sensitivity is far lower.
Pre-commit
Delegate tea pricing authority to a named executive within a pre-agreed band, so that a pass-through decision does not require a committee. The lag is almost always governance, not analysis.
Trigger
Auction prices above the agreed band for two consecutive quarters · pass-through lag exceeding 90 days · value-added mix failing to improve for three quarters.

Scenario four — the bull case, and why it is worth planning for

Likelihood ~20%
Mechanism
The growth portfolio compounds at 35%, the snacks acquisition lands near 3× and integrates cleanly, RTD reaches national distribution ahead of schedule, and the dividend cut is accepted without a shareholder fight. FY31 arrives at roughly ₹49,000 crore with EBITDA above 17%.
The risk in success
Bull cases are usually planned for badly, because organisations prepare for shortfalls and improvise on upside. The binding constraint at 35% growth is not demand or capital — it is capacity and people. A snacks business growing that fast runs out of frying lines and route capacity, and a portfolio compounding at 35% runs out of general managers who have done it before.
Pre-commit
Maintain a standing capacity option — land, long-lead equipment slots, and a co-manufacturing panel — sized for the bull case rather than the base case. Capacity that arrives eighteen months late converts a bull case into a base case, and it is the cheapest insurance in this document.
Trigger
Growth portfolio above 33% for two consecutive quarters · capacity utilisation above 85% in any growth category · more than two GM roles open in the growth portfolio.

Scenario five — you do not acquire at all

The counterfactual
Mechanism
Nothing goes wrong. This is not a downside case — it is the same plan with the acquisitions removed. The growth portfolio still compounds at 28%, core India at 5%, international at 5%, non-branded still exits by FY28. The ₹15,000 crore is simply never deployed: no snacks platform, no regional spice house, no dairy. Every other recommendation in Part Three proceeds unchanged.
Where it lands
₹31,471 crore by FY31 — a 9.2% CAGR. Or ₹33,871 crore at 10.8% if you also keep the non-branded business rather than divesting it. Against a Nestlé bar of ₹40,660 crore, that is not second place; it is roughly ₹9,000 crore behind and out of the race entirely. Modelled
What you avoid
No new goodwill — capital employed is ₹26,063 crore instead of ₹41,063 crore, and the ex-goodwill/statutory split that occupies so much of sections 17 and 19 simply does not arise. There is one ROCE number and it is the real one.
No acquisition dilution — no two-year integration drag on margin, and no integration capacity consumed by an asset while Capital Foods and Organic India are still unfinished.
No snacks mix drag — you are not blending a 12–16% EBITDA category into a portfolio you are trying to take to 16.5%.
No dividend cut — the payout stays near 64% rather than falling to 35%, because the retention it was funding is not needed. That is roughly ₹500 crore a year returned to shareholders instead of deployed at 5%.
What it earns
EBITDA margin lands at 16.4% on the section 17 model's own drivers, and plausibly 17.5% once the acquisition drag and snacks category economics are genuinely absent rather than merely assumed away. On ₹26,063 crore of capital that is a statutory ROCE of 16.6% to 18.0% — against 13.3% in the base case and 14.5% in the stretch case — and, critically, the same number on both measures, because there is no acquisition goodwill to exclude.

Economic profit: +₹1,470 to +₹1,824 crore, against +₹1,437 crore for the stretch case that hits the revenue target. Judgment
The verdict, stated plainly
By revenue rank this is failure — a distant, unambiguous second, and the "number one by FY31" ambition is abandoned. By value per share it may well be the better answer. It generates as much or more economic profit than the case that wins the race, on ₹11,400 crore less revenue, with materially less execution risk, no integration exposure, and the dividend intact. Whether that trade is right is a judgment about what the company is for. It is emphatically the board's judgment to make, not one to be settled by a revenue target adopted before anybody priced the alternative.
Pre-commit
Put this comparison in the board pack alongside the acquisition case, not instead of it. The board will ask for it — the first director who divides ₹15,000 crore by the returns in section 14 will arrive here unaided — and it is far better that you bring it than that they request it. A management team that has already priced its own counterfactual is credible when it then argues for the acquisition; one that has not looks as though it avoided the question.
What would make the acquisition case win anyway
Three things, and they should be argued explicitly rather than assumed: that the snacks position in a category doubling by 2033 is worth more in FY35 than the model's five-year window can show; that entry windows genuinely close, so the option is not available later at any price; and that a company compounding at 9% attracts a materially lower multiple than one compounding at 14%, which matters to shareholders even when economic profit does not move. All three may be true. None of them is currently written down.

The four triggers that should force a change of strategy, not just of tactics

Everything above assumes the strategy holds and only the conditions vary. These four say the strategy itself is wrong and must be reopened. Agree them with the board now, so that reopening the strategy is a pre-authorised act rather than an admission of failure.

  1. Growth portfolio below 25% for two consecutive quarters
    The organic thesis is broken. Two-thirds of the plan depends on this portfolio compounding; if it decelerates, no amount of M&A closes the gap. Response: reset the public ambition immediately and honestly, before the market resets it for you, and redirect capital from acquisitions into fixing the go-to-market.
  2. ROCE flat at around 9% through FY28
    If two years of mix shift, cost programme and capital release have not moved statutory returns, the problem is operating discipline rather than portfolio composition — and adding acquired capital to an organisation that cannot earn returns on the capital it already has makes it strictly worse. Response: stop all M&A. Fix the operating engine first.
  3. Quick commerce above 15% of urban revenue with contribution more than ten points below general trade
    The channel-native reorganisation in section 19 moves from important to urgent, and pack architecture becomes a board-level matter rather than a commercial one. Response: accelerate the structural change; accept a slower category-GM transition to get the channel P&Ls stood up first.
  4. A salty snacks asset of scale transacts to ITC, Reliance or PepsiCo
    The entry window described in section 15 has closed, and the remaining assets will be smaller, more regional and more expensive. Response: do not chase. Redeploy the entire M&A budget to spices, protein and value-added dairy, and reset the FY31 revenue ambition down by roughly ₹6,000 crore. Chasing a closed window at 5× revenue is the most expensive mistake available to you.
How to use this section

Put the four triggers on the monthly scorecard as binary indicators — not as commentary, as flags that are either raised or not. The purpose of a trigger is to remove the discretion that lets an organisation explain away three bad quarters in a row. If a flag goes up, the strategy conversation happens at the next board meeting automatically, whether or not anyone requests it.

The three questions to ask every executive

  1. "If I gave you 30% more capital and 30% more of the best people, what would you do, and what would it return?"
    Tells you whether they have a real plan or a budget. The best answers are specific about returns; the weak ones are specific about activity.
  2. "What in your business should we stop doing, and why haven't we?"
    Tells you whether they can kill their own work, and where the organisation's political blockages are. An executive with no answer either does not know their business or does not trust you yet.
  3. "Who is the competitor that worries you most, and what do they do better than us?"
    Tells you whether they are looking outward. An executive who names only price-based competitors is not thinking hard enough.
One last thing

You are taking over a company that is genuinely performing — 15% revenue growth, 13% volume growth, an innovation engine that works, and the most trusted brand name in Indian food. The temptation in that situation is to change little and let it run. The argument of this document is that the growth is real but the returns are not, that the core is ageing faster than the portfolio is rebalancing, and that the two categories which would most change the company's trajectory — snacking and health — have windows that close within about three years. The case for moving fast is not that something is broken. It is that the timing is unusually good and it will not stay that way.

The method

Each chapter in this part follows the same six steps, because the useful conversations with a CEO are the ones that separate what is known from what is believed:

  1. The questions on the table. Your questions, sharpened where they could be dodged, and joined by the ones an outside-in reviewer would add.
  2. First principles. The underlying economics that decides the answer, stated before the company's position, so the answer can be tested rather than accepted.
  3. What the public record shows. Disclosed facts from the annual report, investor presentations, earnings calls and reputable press, each cited. Most of the hard questions are already partly answered in public; the gaps are where the conversation should go.
  4. The outside-in view. An assessment: where the company is genuinely strong, where the stated position and the evidence diverge, and where nobody outside can yet tell. Judgment
  5. What a strong answer sounds like. Practical suggestions, written as a good answer would be given — specific, measurable and owned.
  6. Follow-ups and evidence to ask for. The second and third questions, which matter more than the first, and the numbers that would settle the point.
Three rules for the conversation itself

Ask for the number, then the mechanism, then the exception. "What is it?", "what drives it?", "where has it not worked?" A leadership team that can answer the third is a team that is learning.

Prefer "how do you know?" to "are you sure?" The first invites evidence; the second invites defence.

Ask what they have stopped doing. Strategy is visible in the trade-offs, not the priorities. Every chapter below has at least one question of this kind.

First principles

An acquisition creates value only if the buyer can do something with the asset that the seller could not, and pays less than that difference. Everything else — strategic fit, portfolio logic, "capabilities" — is a restatement of that sentence or a way of avoiding it. In Indian FMCG specifically, the "something" is almost always one of three things: distribution reach the target could not afford, trust the target could not earn on its own, or cost of capital (patient group money against a founder who needs liquidity). The price has to leave room for the integration to stumble, because in FMCG it usually does: disruption in the first year destroys share that takes three to recover.

The second principle is about time. Paying 6–7× sales for a business earning 10–15% EBITDA means the deal earns low single digits on its capital in the early years by construction. Whether that is a good deal depends entirely on how long the growth lasts — which is why the post-deal review matters more than the pre-deal model.

Category-Defining Brands: the Playbook and the Route

Tata Salt defined a category forty years ago. The question is whether the company has a repeatable way to do it again — and how it is set up to.

First principles

A category-defining brand is one whose name becomes the consumer's shorthand for the category's promise — so that competitors are measured against it. That happens only when three conditions coincide: a real consumer problem the category had not solved (adulteration, inconsistency, effort), a standard the brand sets and keeps (iodisation, "unpolished", restaurant taste at home), and enough availability and repetition that the standard becomes the default. Most "category creation" fails on the third condition, not the first. The economic prize is a sustained price premium with lower marketing cost per unit than followers — the definer pays once to educate the category; followers pay every year to be compared with it.

For Tata specifically, the most valuable version of this is in trust-deficit categories: where consumers cannot verify quality at the point of purchase and fear adulteration. There the Tata name does work no amount of advertising can buy — and that is the CEO's own stated filter57.

First principles

"Disintermediation" is three different risks that are often lumped together:

  1. The distributor is disintermediated — modern trade and quick commerce buy direct. For the brand this is mostly good: fewer hands on the margin. The risk is to the general-trade system that still sells most of the volume, and therefore to the brand's rural and small-town reach.
  2. The brand is disintermediated — the platform's own label replaces it. This is the serious risk, and it is category-specific: it is highest where the product is a commodity the consumer cannot tell apart, and lowest where trust, taste or habit decide.
  3. Discovery is disintermediated — the platform's search ranking and paid placement decide what the shopper sees. This converts brand equity into a rent paid to the platform as retail media.

Channel power follows whoever owns the shopper's moment of choice, the inventory and the data. In general trade the brand owns the first and shares the second; in quick commerce the platform owns all three.

First principles

A matrix exists to share something expensive — a salesforce, a supply chain, a brand — across units that would each be sub-scale alone. Its cost is decision latency and diluted accountability. The matrix pays when the shared asset is worth more than the speed lost, which is true for supply chain and back office almost everywhere, and for the salesforce only when the categories sold together share an outlet and a call pattern. The design rule that makes a matrix work: one dimension owns the P&L, the others own a service level, and every recurring conflict has a named tie-breaker and a deadline.

  • Protected cells: in acquisitions, "the acquired company's core team usually stays separate and protected because it drives much of the brand's value"56.

The company has made the right structural moves: protected teams for acquired brands, a split salesforce for growth brands, and a monthly planning forum (IBP) to resolve supply conflicts. Moats, Network Effects and the Reinvestment Loop

Amul's moat is a cooperative of 36 lakh farmers; ITC's is the largest private agri-sourcing network in India. What is Tata Consumer's — and is efficiency being turned into reinvestment, or into margin?

Read the full chapterCollapse chapter
Questions on the table
  1. How are you building network effects and a moat — as Amul and ITC have?
  2. How are you managing efficiency, and how is that being reinvested?
  3. Added: Which of your advantages would still exist if a competitor had unlimited money — Reliance, say?

First principles

True network effects — where each additional user makes the product better for every other user — are rare in FMCG. What consumer companies build instead are compounding loops: scale lowers cost, lower cost funds price or advertising, which brings more scale. Amul is the closest thing in Indian food to a genuine two-sided network: more farmers bring more milk at lower cost, which funds lower prices and wider reach, which raises farmer realisations and attracts more farmers. ITC's loop runs through sourcing: buying directly from farmers at scale (now through the MAARS platform, with a target of 4,000 farmer producer organisations) feeds Aashirvaad's cost and quality82. A moat is whatever makes such a loop hard to copy.

The efficiency question is the engine of the loop: a rupee of productivity saved is either reinvested in price and brand (the loop compounds) or kept as margin (the loop stalls). Both can be right; it should be a choice, not an accident.

The closest thing to a network effect the company is building is in its distributor and retailer platforms. MAVIC connects 3,000+ distributors; AI in the sales app now influences 41% of orders; a recommendation engine is in pilot3435. If those tools get better with every outlet that uses them — better assortment suggestions, better credit, better schemes — they become a data network that a new entrant cannot buy. Today they are efficiency tools; the question is whether they become a platform.

The group ecosystem is the under-used asset. Starbucks' 500 stores, BigBasket's grocery shoppers and the Tata Neu loyalty programme are first-party consumer touchpoints few FMCG companies have. We are turning our distributor and retailer platforms into a data network — every outlet that orders through it gets better recommendations, and we measure that. Our efficiency rule is explicit: half of each year's productivity savings is reinvested in A&P and price-pack architecture, half goes to margin. And we have a group data partnership with BigBasket and Tata Neu that gives us shopper-level insight our competitors cannot see."

First principles

In FMCG, technology pays through four levers and no others: more revenue per outlet (right assortment, always available), less capital (inventory and receivables), lower cost to serve (routes, dispatch, automation), and faster, better innovation (insight to launch). A programme that cannot name which lever it moves, and by how much, is a cost. On build versus buy: buy the platform, build the edge. Transaction systems, distributor management and planning engines are commodities best bought from scale vendors. The models that encode what only this company knows — which assortment sells in which outlet, how demand responds to price in which town — are where in-house talent earns its cost.

First principles

For a consumer goods company, the expensive cyber event is not a data breach — it is an operational stop: no orders, no invoices, no dispatch, no production.
